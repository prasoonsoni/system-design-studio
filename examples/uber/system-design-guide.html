System Design of Uber

A rider taps Request, and within seconds the platform must pick one driver out of thousands nearby, using positions that are already a few seconds old. It must also make sure that driver is never promised two trips at once and that the fare is charged exactly once. This guide designs that system end to end. The location firehose and the marketplace stay fast and approximate. The trip and the money stay strictly correct.

01

Overview

Uber's ride-hailing product connects riders who want to go somewhere with drivers who are online nearby. The platform quotes an upfront fare and finds and dispatches a driver. It streams the driver's position to the rider during pickup and the trip, then charges the rider and credits the driver's earnings. Everything runs at city scale, in hundreds of cities at once, with traffic peaks on Friday nights, at concert exits and in rainstorms.

Governing insight

Dispatch is real-time geospatial matching over positions that are stale the moment they arrive. Treat location as a disposable AP stream, and treat the trip and its payment as the only strongly consistent truth. A driver can hold at most one live offer, enforced by a lease, never by a fresh read of location.

Two consequences follow. First, the hottest data in the system (where every driver is right now) never touches a durable database on the request path. It lives in memory, sharded by geography, and is rebuilt from the stream if a node dies. Second, the correctness-critical writes (trip transitions, driver assignment, ledger postings) are few by comparison, about 14k/s at peak against 375k location pings/s. They can afford compare-and-set and consensus-backed storage.

In scope

  • Upfront fare quote and ride request (UberX-style, single rider)
  • Driver online/offline, location ingestion, supply index
  • Dispatch: candidate search, ranking by ETA, offers, accept/decline
  • Trip lifecycle, live tracking, cancellation
  • Surge (dynamic pricing) per hexagon
  • Payment capture, double-entry ledger, receipts, ratings

Out of scope (external or separate)

  • Card networks and processors, wallets (external PSPs)
  • Map tile rendering and basemap data curation
  • Driver onboarding and background checks (treated as a status flag)
  • Uber Eats, freight, shared rides (pooling), scheduled rides
  • Driver payouts to bank accounts (consumes the ledger)

Takeaway: each plane scales on a different axis

The AP location plane scales on raw write rate (375k/s) and socket count (4.6M). Keep positions in memory, shard by geography and never put them on a consensus path. The CP trip plane handles about 26× fewer writes (14k/s) but each one needs compare-and-set and durable replication. The money plane has the lowest rate (about 1.4k ledger rows/s) and the highest value per write, so it gets append-only storage and daily reconciliation.

04

Architecture

Edge

The API Gateway terminates TLS and authenticates OAuth2 access tokens. It enforces per-user rate limits, checks the Idempotency-Key header and routes request/response calls. The Realtime Gateway holds one long-lived WebSocket per app. It carries driver location pings inbound and offers, trip updates and driver positions outbound. It is stateless apart from its connections: a session registry in Redis maps user_id → gateway node, so any service can publish to a user. When a socket drops, the app falls back to push notifications plus polling GET /v1/trips/{id}.

Trip and money plane (CP)

The Trip Service owns the trip state machine and is the single writer of a trip row. Every transition is a compare-and-set on (trip_id, version) in Docstore. Uber describes Docstore as giving strict serializability per partition through Raft and favouring consistency over availability [2]. Dispatch runs matching and owns the driver lease. Fare Quote freezes a price into a short-lived quote. Payments posts to an append-only double-entry ledger. Post-trip work runs as a Cadence workflow, which retries durably for minutes or days without anyone holding a lock. Cadence is Uber's open-source workflow engine [4].

Location and marketplace plane (AP)

Location Ingest validates and normalizes pings (snaps to H3, rejects impossible jumps) and publishes them to driver.location, keyed by driver_id. The Supply Index is an in-memory, H3-sharded map of available drivers. It is built from that topic and spread over nodes with consistent hashing, as Uber's Ringpop library did for its dispatch tier [3]. ETA and Routing answers road-network travel times. Surge is a streaming job that computes supply and demand per hexagon. Uber's H3 post says surge is calculated from supply and demand in hexagons [1].

Event backbone

Kafka decouples producers from consumers and is replayable. Its five topics are listed in §07. Everything that does not have to block the rider runs off the bus: notifications, fraud scoring, the analytics lake, location history. Producers use idempotent writes, and consumers dedupe on event_id, so at-least-once delivery behaves like effectively-once processing.

05

Component deep-dives

Location Ingest & Supply Index

AP
Responsibility
Know where every online driver is, to within one ping, and answer "available drivers near this point" in under 10 ms.
How it works

Each ping (lat, lng, heading, speed, accuracy, ts) is converted to an H3 cell at resolution 9 (~0.1 km² hexagons) and published to Kafka. H3 indexes a point at a resolution from 0 to 15 [1]. Supply Index nodes own resolution-5 parent cells, so a city's districts spread across many nodes while a neighbourhood stays on one. Each node keeps cell_r9 → {driver_id → (pos, ts, status, product)}.

A query for a pickup takes the pickup's r9 cell and expands a k-ring (k = 1, 2, 3…) until it has enough candidates or reaches a radius cap. Hexagons have uniform neighbour distance, which makes ring expansion clean. When a ring crosses a shard boundary, the node scatter-gathers to the neighbouring owner.

Data owned
In-memory index only (rebuildable). Last-known driver positions are mirrored to Redis with a 30 s TTL for other readers.
Failure handling
When a node dies, the hash ring reassigns its cells, and the new owner replays the last ~30 s of driver.location from Kafka to warm up. A driver with no ping for 15 s is marked stale and excluded from matching. Pings with impossible speed (>250 km/h) or mock-location flags go to Fraud.

Dispatch (matching + offers)

CP leaseAP reads
Responsibility
Turn a DISPATCHING trip into an accepted driver, fast, without ever offering one driver two trips.
How it works

1. Candidates: k-ring query to the Supply Index, filtered by product, vehicle and driver status AVAILABLE, and also by drivers about to finish a nearby trip.

2. Rank: batch ETA call (road-network time, not straight-line distance), plus marketplace terms such as driver idle time and acceptance likelihood.

3. Batch match (assumption): instead of greedy first-come matching, collect requests in a city zone for a short window (~1–2 s) and solve an assignment that minimizes total pickup ETA. Greedy matching gives the nearest driver to the first rider even when a second rider was a better fit.

4. Lease and offer: CAS the driver's state in Redis from AVAILABLE to OFFERED with lease_until = now + 15s and offer_id. Only after the CAS wins is the offer pushed via dispatch.offers. On decline, timeout or lease expiry, release the driver and try the next candidate.

Data owned
driver:state:{driver_id} lease in Redis (replicated, AOF on). DispatchOffer rows persisted asynchronously for audit and acceptance-rate metrics.
Failure handling
Dispatch workers are stateless per city zone. If one dies mid-offer, the lease expires in 15 s and the trip's dispatch timer (owned by Trip Service) re-emits trip.dispatch_retry. The lease is the safety property: even if two workers process the same trip, only one CAS on a given driver wins, and the trip transition is a second CAS.

Trip Service

CP
Responsibility
Source of truth for a trip, from request to final fare. It is the only writer of the trip row.
How it works
REQUESTED→DISPATCHING→DRIVER_ASSIGNED→DRIVER_ARRIVED→IN_PROGRESS→COMPLETED·CANCELEDNO_DRIVERS

Each command (accept, arrive, start, complete, cancel) is validated against the current state. It is written as UPDATE … SET state, version = version + 1 WHERE trip_id = ? AND version = ? together with a TripEvent row in the same partition transaction, then published to trip.events through a transactional outbox. An illegal transition (start before arrive) returns 409.

Data owned
Trip, TripEvent, outbox, in Docstore partitioned by trip_id. A secondary index on (rider_id, created_at) and (driver_id, created_at) serves trip history.
Failure handling
The outbox relay guarantees that every committed transition is eventually published, at least once. Docstore's Raft groups survive the loss of a replica. If a partition loses quorum, writes to those trips fail fast with 503 and the apps retry with the same idempotency key.

Fare Quote & Surge

AP
Responsibility
Give the rider a price they can trust before they request, and nudge supply toward demand.
How it works

Surge is a stream job over driver.location (supply) and trip.events plus quote requests (demand), with tumbling 1-minute windows per H3 cell (resolution 7 or 8 here, an assumption). Each cell's multiplier is smoothed against its neighbours so prices don't jump across a street, capped, and written to surge:{city}:{h3} with a 2-minute TTL. It is also published on marketplace.surge for the driver heat map.

Quote = route ETA and distance from Routing, base + per-km + per-minute rate card, × the surge multiplier at the pickup cell, plus fees and taxes. The result is frozen into quote:{quote_id} with a 5-minute TTL and an HMAC signature, so the request can't tamper with the price.

Data owned
Rate cards (config DB), quote cache, surge cache. Every multiplier is also archived to the lake for audit and regulatory questions.
Failure handling
If surge is stale or missing, use the last value until the TTL expires, then fall back to 1.0×. It never fails a quote. If Routing is down, estimate from straight-line distance × a city detour factor and flag the quote as estimated.

ETA & Routing

AP
Responsibility
Point-to-point and one-to-many travel times for quotes, dispatch ranking and live pickup ETA.
How it works
A road-graph router (contraction hierarchies or similar, which is an assumption) with live traffic speeds per segment gives a base ETA. An ML model then corrects that ETA. Uber has published its DeepETA work on post-processing routing ETAs with deep learning [7]. Dispatch calls a matrix endpoint (N drivers × 1 pickup) in a single request.
Data owned
The road graph (read-only, versioned) and segment speeds, rebuilt from aggregated trip traces.
Failure handling
Stateless replicas per region. The model runs behind a timeout, with the raw router ETA as the fallback.

Payments & Ledger

CP
Responsibility
Move money correctly: pre-authorize, capture, refund, and record every cent as balanced ledger entries.
How it works

On request, place a pre-authorization hold with the PSP for the quoted fare plus a buffer. On COMPLETED, a Cadence workflow runs these steps: compute final fare → capture (with an idempotency key of trip_id:capture at the PSP) → post a journal whose entries sum to zero → emit payment.events → receipt.

Example journal for a $20.00 fare: debit rider_receivable 20.00 · credit driver_earnings 15.20 · credit platform_fee 3.60 · credit tax_payable 1.20. Amounts are integer minor units plus an ISO 4217 currency code. Floats are never used.

Data owned
Payment, LedgerEntry (append-only, never updated), PaymentMethod (PSP token only, never a card number).
Failure handling
A capture failure retries with backoff, then tries the rider's backup method, then records rider arrears and blocks the next request until it is settled. The trip itself is never rolled back. Daily reconciliation matches PSP settlement files to ledger captures.

Realtime Gateway & Notifications

AP
Responsibility
Hold 4.6M sockets and deliver offers, trip updates and driver positions in under a second.
How it works
It consumes dispatch.offers and trip.events, looks up the target's node in the session registry and forwards the message. For live tracking, the gateway serving a rider subscribes to that trip's driver position, which is fanned out from driver.location through a lightweight per-trip pub/sub channel. Every message carries a monotonic seq, and the client ignores anything older than what it has already shown.
Data owned
Session registry (session:{user_id} → node, TTL refreshed by heartbeat).
Failure handling
A node drain sends a reconnect frame, and clients reconnect with jitter. Offers are critical, so if the socket is absent the offer also goes out as a high-priority push through APNs/FCM.

Sync vs async boundary

The rider's request is synchronous only as far as a durable REQUESTED row. Steps 1–3 are sync and return 202. Matching (4–6) runs async through Kafka and pushes the result over the socket, because it can take 2–30 s and must survive app restarts. Accept (step 6) is synchronous again so the driver gets an immediate yes or no on the race. Everything after completion (step 11) is async and retried durably. The rider never waits on a card processor.

Idempotency layers

  • Edge: idem:{user}:{key} stores the request hash and response, so a replay returns the stored response.
  • Domain: Trip.idempotency_key UNIQUE, plus a one-active-trip-per-rider check. Accept is idempotent per offer_id.
  • Events: consumers keep a processed event_id set (or apply only if version > last_seen).
  • Money: PSP idempotency key per capture and Payment.idempotency_key UNIQUE. Reconciliation catches whatever slips through. There is no exactly-once claim, only idempotent effects plus a daily proof.

Hot spots and spikes

  • Event surges (a stadium lets out, New Year's Eve): demand concentrates in a handful of cells. Pre-split those cells' shards ahead of known events, widen the dispatch batch window slightly so more requests share one solve, and let surge rise to bring drivers in. Queue requests with a visible "high demand" state instead of rejecting them.
  • Airports: model them as a FIFO driver queue in a geofenced lot, not k-ring proximity. It is fairer to drivers and cheaper to compute.
  • Location write rate: adaptive ping interval (every 4 s while moving, slower while parked, faster during pickup approach) cuts load by roughly a third.
  • Read-heavy quotes: cache route ETAs per (origin r9, destination r9, 5-min bucket). Many riders in one building quote similar trips.
  • Autoscaling: every service except stores is stateless and scales on CPU or connection count, with predictive scale-up from the weekly demand curve per city.

Security & compliance

Identity & access

  • OAuth2 with short-lived access tokens and refresh rotation. Phone OTP plus device binding.
  • Service-to-service mTLS with per-service identities and authorization policy at the mesh.
  • Object-level checks: a driver can act only on a trip where driver_id = sub.
  • Periodic driver selfie verification against onboarding documents (account-sharing defence).

Data protection

  • Card data never touches our services. The app tokenizes with the PSP, keeping us in a reduced PCI DSS scope.
  • Phone numbers are masked through a calling proxy, so riders and drivers never see each other's numbers.
  • Precise location is PII: encrypted at rest, access logged and justified, raw traces expired on a retention schedule (assumed 90 days raw).
  • Data subject access and erasure requests per GDPR/CCPA. Trip financial records are kept for the tax retention period.

Abuse & safety

  • GPS spoofing: mock-location flags, impossible speeds, and mismatches between GPS, cell and Wi-Fi positions feed a fraud score. Inflated-distance trips are re-priced from the trusted trace.
  • Promo and referral fraud: device fingerprinting and payment instrument graphs.
  • Trip anomalies: long unexpected stops or route deviations trigger an in-app check-in. Uber publicly describes this kind of feature as RideCheck.
  • Rate limiting at the gateway, plus a separate budget on quote endpoints to stop price scraping.

Trade-offs

DecisionChoice & why
Geo indexH3 hexagons over geohash or quadtree. Uniform neighbour distance gives clean k-ring expansion and fair surge cells, and Uber built it for exactly this [1]. Cost: harder to reason about than lat/lng boxes.
Where locations liveIn memory, rebuilt from Kafka, not a database. 375k writes/s of data that expires in seconds doesn't deserve durability. Cost: a warm-up gap after a shard failure.
Matching strategyShort-window batch matching over greedy nearest-driver. It lowers total pickup time across a zone. Cost: 1–2 s of added latency and a zone-local solver.
Preventing double bookingDriver lease (CAS + TTL) plus a trip version CAS, not a distributed lock service. The TTL self-heals crashed workers.
Trip storeDocstore-like, CP per partition [2]. Correctness beats availability for the trip. A quorum loss fails a few trips' writes briefly instead of corrupting state.
Request API202 + push over a long-held HTTP request. Matching can take 30 s and has to survive app backgrounding.
Post-trip money flowDurable workflow (Cadence) over ad-hoc retries and cron jobs. Each step is visible, retryable and resumable [4].
PricingUpfront, signed quote over a metered fare. The rider sees a firm price, and the platform takes the variance risk.
If you remember five things
  1. Split the planes: locations, ETA and surge are AP; trip, lease and ledger are CP.
  2. Index drivers in H3 cells in memory and search outward with k-rings; never query a database for "nearby".
  3. A driver is offered one trip at a time, enforced by a TTL lease CAS, with a trip-version CAS as the backstop.
  4. Request returns 202 once the trip is durable, matching runs async, and results come back over WebSocket.
  5. Money runs as a durable workflow into a double-entry ledger, with idempotency keys and daily reconciliation, never "exactly once".
